In NLP, methods like [word2vec](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1301.3781) quantify words into vectors called embeddings that represent their meanings numerically across a set of dimensions. These embeddings are derived from how words appear with each other across a large training set, because context is what gives words meaning in the abstract space of natural language. With enough training, these embedding models learn that the vector representation resulting from subtracting 'man' from 'king' and adding 'woman' can also represent 'queen', providing a reasonable numerical proxy for meaning we assign to words.

In genetics, we can apply similar methods to numerically represent regions of the genome with biological significance, like those that are transcription factor binding sites or physically accessible to regulatory mechanisms. But what might the resulting embedding vectors per region actually encode? Natural language is metaphysical and often ambiguous in nature, while regulatory genomics is grounded in highly specific physical mechanisms that involve positional distances, physical conformations, and chemical specificities that all play a role in determining biological function. Does the genomic region embedding space as produced by our lab's pretrained [region2vec](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btab439) model encode meaning in a way that can be explored as a lexicon representing physical biology?

## Regions as words

The ENCODE project is a long running aggregation of experimental results for functional genomics. With its data, the project so far has identified almost a million human candidate cis-Regulatory Elements (cCREs), each annotated as promoter-like, enhancer-like, DNase-H3K4me3, or CTCF-only in a cell-type agnostic manner. Most experimental results are stored in BED files, where the regions associated with a specific outcome are tabulated together. For example, BED files from an experiment investigating accessible regions for K562 leukemia cells would contain a list of all regions by chromosome and start/end positions that were measured to be accessible.

If we treat genomic regions like words, these BED files are our documents, and the context embedding models learn is the way in which regions co-occur in them across experimental results. We shuffle the order of regions per BED file during training so that the models learn meaning and not mere positional ordering of regions. With enough training context, region2vec is able to distinguish clusters of regions by cCRE annotation class, as seen in region embeddings for chromosome 11. Conversely, position along the chromosome shows very little structure, which is the result of shuffling the regions within BED files during training. With BED file frequency, we see a gradient rather than resolving into groups, showing that the model is distinctively learning co-occurrence patterns in the data.

## Cell lines within the distal enhancer cluster

We can see visual subclusters within the mass of distal enhancer-like regions on the left. If these embeddings encode any biology beyond our existing annotations and features, we should start with cell types. A region that is accessible in leukemia cells is not necessarily accessible in liver cells, and the ENCODE corpus spans hundreds of biosamples. If we score each region by how strongly it belongs to K562 leukemia cells, HepG2 liver cells, or GM12878 lymphoblastoid cells, and then color the same embedding space by that score, the cluster separates, with each cell line taking its own portion of what the annotation treats as a single class.

Enhancers differing between cell lines is not really surprising, since being active in some cells and not others is close to what defines an enhancer. But does this separation correspond to anything deeper biologically? Transcription factor binding gives us a way to check.

![The chromosome 11 embedding space in five rows and three columns, one column per cell line, colored by cell line specificity and by the regions each category of transcription factor binds](/figures/dictionary/fig2_cell_type.webp "The same chromosome 11 embedding as above. Each column is one cell line, and rows 3-5 use the same transcription factors across columns. Cell line specificity and identity factor binding shift between cell lines, while CTCF and cohesin are present in the same cluster in all three.")

The transcription factors that define each cell line fall within that cell line's portion of the embedding space. GATA1, GATA2, TAL1, and NFE2 drive red blood cell identity and mark regions within the K562 portion, HNF4A, HNF1A, FOXA1, and FOXA2 do the same for liver in HepG2, and PAX5, EBF1, and IKZF1 for B cells in GM12878. The lower rows act as a control, since they use the same list of transcription factors across cell lines. General machinery and signal-responsive factors still shift between cell lines, while CTCF and cohesin occupy the same cluster of regions in all three, which is consistent with their role in maintaining chromatin structure across many cell types rather than specifying cell identity.

## The arrangement of transcription factors

The regions themselves are not shared between cell lines. If we take the same transcription factors and ask whether they bind the same regions in two different cell lines, the overlap is roughly half for CTCF and cohesin and less than a tenth for identity. But the spatial arrangement of the transcription factors relative to each cell line shows more consistency. If we consider the centroids of each transcription factor or category, meaning the average embedding position of all regions it binds, we can use cosine angles between those directions to measure their similarity.

![Pairwise alignment of fourteen transcription factors in the embedding space, and the four categories drawn as directions, one column per cell line](/figures/dictionary/fig3_factor_arrangement.webp "The top row shows how aligned each pair of fourteen transcription factors is in the embedding space. Transcription factors are ordered by category. The bottom row shows the four categories as directions in two SVD components that preserve most of their separation.")

Each cell line has 91 pairwise similarities between the fourteen transcription factors we have in our set. Comparing these pairwise similarities between cell lines gives a mean correlation of +0.90, indicating strong consistency in relative representations of biological function. Removing CTCF and RAD21 leaves 66 pairs, and the correlation falls to +0.53. This is still well above what shuffling the transcription factor labels results in (0.00 ± 0.15), so the other twelve do hold a shared arrangement on their own.

While the regions themselves are distinct between cell lines, this shows that the model is able to pick up on underlying patterns of biological mechanisms they are involved in regardless of the region's identity or position. This is like natural language in some ways. Two languages can share almost none of their vocabulary and still be organized in similar ways internally due to the relationships between words being consistent across them. Here, the words differ from one cell line to the next, while the relationships between them are shared.

## Deriving meaning from embeddings

What embedding models essentially do for natural language is quantify the web of relationships between human-defined words from a large corpus of documents. The representations these models and LLMs learn are not novel, but simply mappings of a conceptual space that we implicitly define over many millions of unique but patterned ways. They do not create new words and define new relationships between them, but reveal what we know and don't know about how we use language in a way that allows us to physically measure these concepts. Likewise, region2vec and our work so far with genomic regions do not synthesize new biology, but reveal the existing co-occurrence patterns of genomic regions in a way that allows us to map them out, and ask what biological mechanisms and features may really be involved.

The embeddings themselves invite a lot of questions. When co-occurrence patterns result in maps like the ones shown here, how and in what contexts are regions with different mechanistic roles but similar dimensions co-occurring? Both natural language and our lab have moved onto [transformer-based approaches](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.11.03.685753) to capture multiple independent contexts, like in the case of a region being used for a certain mechanism in one cell line but different mechanisms in other cell lines. What about the fidelity of the genomic regions themselves? We use binary ranges along the chromosome as regions, but the regions themselves are aggregations of sequencing signal with varying shapes and peaks, and messy start and end positions across target regions before we consider anything exceeding a threshold as a 'region'. The variable shape or strength of signal at a region could very likely accompany different contexts and contain key information the models can learn from. What about chromosome conformations, do the embeddings encode anything about conformational proximities if not positional? The questions are almost boundless, but the key is to slow down, and work with each of them one at a time.
