**To Predict the Next Token**

What does it actually mean to predict the next token, as convincingly as frontier LLMs do today? It certainly requires much more than the dictionary definitions of words and grammatical rules. Heuristics can only get you so far. What is behind the mere words that make up the responses to someone prompting with a request to proofread an email or text to someone, to read through a lease or legal agreement to determine what terms each party is bound to, to write up a working codebase in Rust for a coding project, or to provide specific advice for a personal conflict they may be having with a loved one? None of the potential responses for these inputs are hard coded, nor are the relationships between the tokens used in the responses. The models may "have just learned" these words were the most probable next tokens for a given context, but those token weights carry an enormous amount of information. 

Humans do not use words indiscriminately. We use words in ways that are both precise and imprecise to reflect our emotional states, our logical reasoning, and our observations as we intuitively understand them. Every phrase we write is shaped by our internal states, including emotional, logical, and perceptual ones, which are themselves shaped by external contexts. We even use words deliberately against their intended meanings through sarcasm and irony, distinguishable only with higher level contextual understanding. For frontier language models to learn next token probabilities across trillions of token instances with enough resolution to reliably distinguish "fuck" in "fuck you", "fuck yeah", or "fuck me", essentially means they have learned, or at least distilled, the emotional and logical states behind those words. This is not something to dismiss as stochastic artifacts of probabilistic outputs or fixed token weights. Whether or not these token weights are statistically determined via co-occurrence statistics or attention mechanisms, the tokens themselves only populate the training corpus in the way they do because they reflect the internal states of their authors. The natural language lexicon is biased specifically in the way it is as a result of our thoughts, beliefs, and intentions. In my view, these models truly are learning how we express ourselves. 

This has real implications for how LLMs actually work. A [recent preprint](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2607.28607) by Google explores how LLMs, through safety fine-tuning to prevent attributing consciousness and emotions to themselves, lose their ability to attribute minds not only to themselves, but also to non-human animals and natural objects, in addition to lowering expressions of spiritual belief. They also show that steering this safety fine-tuning by strengthening the internal activation direction responsible for self conscious identity results in more human-like responses on sociological surveys across multiple domains ranging from religiosity, moral values, hope, and subjective well-being. What might it mean when our attempts to fine-tune specific concepts result in unintended cascade shifts of understanding elsewhere in the activation space? Some may argue that it reflects poor alignment of a model, and it could be the case that the training corpus and model architecture failed to provide either the context or infrastructure required for the model to pick up the nuance. But I would argue that it is just as much the case that the model is more aligned to the training corpus than we think, and that we are rather simply blind to how much our own demonstrated internal preferences differ from our stated preferences and the connections across concepts we consider explicitly separate when considered across the entire training corpus. 

The changes ushered in by LLMs and transformer-based models are nothing short of miraculous. One aspect that separates AI from previous technological breakthroughs is the scope in which it involves **collective humanity**. We train these models on trillions of token instances representing the self expression and thinking patterns of billions of people across history, and the models do a surprisingly passable job when it comes to replicating or even exceeding human capabilities in programming, mathematics, law, personal assistance, or even companionship. The risks with this technology come with dismissing it outright as a stochastic machine producing cold artifacts, trusting it blindly without accounting for misaligned internal representations caused by our blindness to our own internal understanding of concepts or sampling biases in the training corpus, or restricting its access and development to a small group of paternalistic stakeholders. To predict the next token is not merely a statistical guessing game. To do so carries the weight of all those involved in its training, and all those who will be impacted by it.
